Good morning. Let us understand a principle today, a single rule. The name of that rule is change. Change is happening continuously.

After our meditation, Hanumanji sometimes comes up here and dances. He does not disturb me while I meditate, and later, when we are doing our spiritual practice, none of these things ever disturb us. So, everything in the outer world changes. This body is related to the outer world. This mind is related to the outer world.

In spiritual terminology, in spiritual science, we say one thing: whatever changes, whatever is subject to change, is all Maya. It is not truth. Whatever changes is not the truth. Whatever remains unchanging, whatever exists forever, that alone is truth. How do we find out what is true and what is untrue? This is called a litmus test. How can we discover it? If it changes, it is not truth. It stays for a while and then departs—that is all. This body stays and then departs. This physical frame is cremated. The body decays. It does not exist forever.

We are living in this house right now. The soul dwells inside the house called this body. It is subject to change. Are we right now the same as when we were children? Everything related to it changes—age comes, old age comes, illness comes. It is subject to change. Whatever is subject to change is not truth, because meditation practice is solely for the quest of truth. We must discover what the truth is. We say Satyameva Jayate—truth alone triumphs.

Therefore, there is something that is not subject to change, something that undergoes no transformation at all. That is what we must catch hold of. We observe this during meditation. So many changes occur within our body. Sensations arise and pass away; sensations arise and pass away. We feel light, and then we feel heavy. Light, then heavy. Pleasant sensations arise, or sometimes sorrowful sensations arise. Thoughts are just the same. They keep changing. The thought you have right now will not be there in the next moment. That too changes. That is why the mind is not us, and this body is not us. We reside within the body.

Suppose you are in your house. You built a house; there are walls, there is everything. That house is not you. You are merely dwelling in that house. One day you will leave that house and go into another. We do not remain forever. Whatever is continuously subject to change is not us. That which exists forever without any change is the true me; that is the soul. This is the litmus test. Through this alone we find out.

So, when we practice meditation, when we do sadhana, we recognize so many things within our inner being and grasp all these principles. The pains we had before—as we go deeper and deeper into meditation, as we move into our inner core, those pains vanish. Earlier, there used to be so much shivering. Many people sneeze and cough. In the beginning, that was my observation; I noticed it. But later on in meditation, it changed. It stopped. There was no more sneezing, no more coughing. Why did it change? It came and it went.

Some things keep coming and going. Anything related to this body is subject to change. Anything related to the mind is subject to change. Anything related to the plane of emotions is subject to change. There are good feelings and bad feelings. Auspicious emotions—meaning emotions and feelings—arise, and then they depart as well, and others take their place.

So, anyone in the outer world who expects everything to remain without any change is simply deluding themselves. They think, Oh, why is this changing like this? It should not change. For instance, a mother has immense love for her child. She says, You look so cute; do not grow up at all. Just stay like this forever, like a small child. May your innocence and purity remain just like that forever; never grow up. Would that be good? It would not be good. It is subject to change. That little infant has to grow up into an adult. They develop. It is subject to change.

Here, uncertainty always prevails. What exists on the physical plane? Uncertainty. Uncertainty means that nothing remains fixed. Nothing here happens the way we plan it. We often say:

“Man proposes, God disposes.”

It is always like that. We think of one thing, but things happen in an entirely different way. Yet we must be able to accept it. If we resist change, if we fight against change—Why did it rain today? Why is the temperature so high? It should not be like this. Why is it so cold? It should not be—if we lay down rules like that, if we resist, if we fight it, we get stuck. Problems arise. Uncertainty is everywhere here. We must completely accept this uncertainty. The way of the world is that it keeps changing and changing. People change.

Those who praise you today may start scolding you tomorrow. Those who scolded you may praise you again. People change. No one stays with certainty. Uncertainty, uncertainty. Money is here today, and it goes away tomorrow. It keeps coming and going. You attained great name and fame, but that too will leave. Fame comes and goes. Nothing is predictable. The physical plane simply means uncertainty. It remains just like that.

That is why, first of all, we must cultivate the quality and the endurance to accept any change, because on this physical plane things happen in that very way. Everything keeps changing. We must accept change. We must remain firmly established in our soul. We must abide constantly in that state of equanimity. A yogi, a meditator, revels in that state. That state of stillness never wavers. Your soul is said to be self-born—Swayambhu.

The soul has no birth and no death. It was never born, and it never dies. The soul has always existed. The soul has no birth and no death. We just come here to this globe and depart again—that is all. We come, and we go. But the soul has no birth and no death. The soul transcends all of this.

So, the first principle we need to learn today is that change is utterly natural on this physical plane. We must completely accept that change. We must completely accept the uncertainty present here. We must not cling to it, thinking, This should not change, that should not change. We will one day have to leave this body. We do not stay in this body forever. We must be ready for that change as well. We must be ready for every change.

Many people are here with you now, but later they will not be. If we desire that they must always be with us, that is not how it works. It simply never happens; it is impossible. This is the trouble most people face on the physical plane. They resist. They resist change.

We must be able to accept all changes. Whether money is present or absent, whether a position of authority is held or lost, whether people praise you or criticize you, whether auspicious things happen or inauspicious ones—we must accept all changes. Not everything happens the way we want it to on the physical plane.

Now, starting from today, let us practice awareness deeply. In meditation, awareness is very much present. We keep our attention on the breath, and then we observe with awareness what is happening inside. Now our eyes are open. We get immersed in our daily activities. Now we must increase that awareness. Awareness exists on many levels. Awareness means mindfulness. In an even more beautiful way, Lord Buddha explained it:

“Whatever work you are doing, be completely immersed in that work.”

When you are having breakfast, do not entertain any thoughts at that time. If you are walking, you must be immersed in it. Your complete attention should be on your walking—how your stride falls, how your foot touches Mother Earth. Your attention, your awareness, your mindfulness remains entirely upon that.

So, we must recognize certain things through awareness. There was a Buddhist master named Nagarjuna. He is regarded as one of the greatest Buddhist masters and the greatest philosophers, because so many of the doctrines he expounded remain authoritative for everyone to this day. The texts he wrote in Buddhist literature are still studied in Buddhist universities.

So, one day, a thief came to his ashram. He had great fondness for Nagarjuna. He used to follow the guru silently, observe everything, and listen. People kept talking about how this master guides people wonderfully in meditation, and how great he was. One day, he felt like going to meet him. He went and met the guru.

Right upon meeting him, he blurted out a few things. Just as you should never tell lies to a doctor and should not hide anything—telling the doctor everything you have so that diagnosis is easy—similarly, when you go to a guru, you should not hide anything inside; you must tell the guru things exactly as they are. So the thief said:

“Guruji, Guruji, I am a thief. But my past experience is that whenever I go to any ashram, the gurus always ask me to first give up thievery. But I am unable to give it up because it is my nature. Thievery is not just my profession; it is my very nature. The moment I wake up in the morning, I feel like stealing. What should I do? My only request is—please do not tell me to change this profession. Let me remain a thief, but teach me whatever meditation practice you teach. Impart knowledge to me.”

Then Nagarjuna replied, clearing his throat:

“Alright. Who said anything about pulling you away from your profession? Go ahead and steal nicely. But I will give you a single practice, a sadhana you must perform. That is the sadhana of awareness—mindfulness practice. Even when you go to commit theft, when you go to a house and break through the wall and step inside, you must do every single thing with awareness. Okay? Later, when you locate where the ornaments, gold, or money are hidden, you must go there with awareness and pick them up. You must do every single thing with awareness. You must not do anything without awareness.”

With that constant mindfulness, you must become completely immersed in the movement, he was told. The thief thought, Well, what is this? What the guru said is very nice. I will give him feedback later. I must practice this first.

So, starting the next day, the thief got ready. The thief now had awareness too. Wanting to practice awareness thoroughly, he tried to go and do his work nicely. But when there is awareness, thievery simply disappears! The moment awareness arises in him, his thief-like nature vanishes. When there is no awareness, he feels like mechanically turning into a thief. He tried. Every day he would go to break a wall with awareness. As he raised the hammer to break the wall, the feeling—I should not steal—would arise. When awareness was present, he was not a thief. When awareness was absent, he became a thief.

So he came back, poor fellow; he struggled a lot. Forty days passed. He said:

“Guruji, this is very difficult. I cannot remain a thief. I no longer feel any urge to steal. I tried and tried.”

The guru gave him an option:

“You have the choice. You have the option. If you want to steal, steal. If you want to practice awareness, practice awareness. Choose for yourself.”

The thief replied:

“Guruji, I will stay on the side of awareness. I am practicing awareness.”

Then the guru taught him meditation as well—how to turn inward.

Here, starting from today, we are in this sadhana of awareness. We must remain in awareness even in the outer world, constantly recognizing and observing everything happening within us. First, in our body—because the body is the primary instrument for spiritual practice. Without this body, there is no sadhana. This is a magnificent tool for us, a magnificent instrument. Only if the body exists do we exist here, does the soul reside here, and can we perform spiritual practices like meditation. This body is important. We must keep observing everything happening in this body.

Sometimes hunger arises in our stomach, and if we lack awareness, we do not recognize it. We do not have awareness until it becomes severe enough to cause acidity. Sometimes the body is exhausted, and we must be able to recognize it with awareness. We must be able to recognize every change occurring in the body. While walking, what happens in our body? Where are sensations arising in our body? We must keep recognizing all these. In awareness, first is the body.

Second is the mind. When the mind is constantly in the outer world, it keeps generating thoughts. Do we look at an object? Immediately thoughts arise. When we look at a tree, all the things we have heard about that tree since childhood rush into our thoughts. It does not stop with merely looking at that tree; thoughts about it arise. Oh, there is a mango tree at my house too. This is very big; these mangoes are good. These leaves are wonderful. These flowers are nice. Look how many birds it gives shade to. Such thoughts keep pouring in. We must always remain with awareness of what is happening in the mind.

Then thirdly, feelings. Now, we interact with so many people. Conversing with them, their behaviors and their words tend to influence you; they have an impact on you. They spoke rather harshly to you; they behaved a little strictly with you. Okay? Immediately a feeling arises inside you. Ah, why did he say that? Why did she say this like that? A feeling arises within you, right? You must have awareness. You must observe the feeling.

Sometimes a reaction arises within us. In the spur of the moment, anger arises—a mild irritation, we call it. That mild anger sometimes turns into major anger, and we express it. But if awareness is present, this does not happen. It does not come out. Awareness does that right on the spot—whatever we become conscious of, whatever awareness is there, it completes it right then and there. That experience dissolves. The anger within us dissolves right in that moment. If awareness is present, it simply does not arise.

This is what scientists who discovered quantum physics call the observer-participant principle. At the atomic level, there are molecules, and inside the molecules—if we look to see what is inside—electrons are constantly revolving. Electrons are sub-atomic particles. If we go deeper and deeper inside our body cells, there are the tiniest micro-particles within atoms. These electrons revolve. There are protons and neutrons too. Inside each atom, there is said to be a vast universe. They keep revolving.

Scientists noticed that because these electrons revolve, they create an energy field. We have a department called chemistry—the study of chemicals and material substances. In it, we study many elemental substances: hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and their natures. They have electrons inside.

So scientists performed an experiment called the double-slit experiment. They projected these electrons through a small aperture onto a screen. When they did that, the electrons went and hit the screen randomly. But when an observer was present—when someone watched as a witness—it behaved differently! It behaved according to the observer's expectation.

The nature of the electron has two aspects: material nature or energy nature—wave nature and particle nature. Scientists observed both these behaviors. Sometimes, depending on the observer, depending on the observer's thought process—if this observer, this witness, thinks that the electron is purely matter and has no energy, that it is not wave nature but material nature, that it has a dense nature—then that is exactly how it behaves.

That means the observer is influencing it. The one who observes changes everything. Observation is important; awareness is very important. Whatever you observe within yourself, whatever comes into your awareness—if it is negative, it leaves right then and there, because we are always progressive people. We constantly strive to move from the state we are in to a higher state.

Therefore, whatever awareness arises within you—you got angry and you observed it with awareness, saying, Anger is arising in me, anger has come—you noticed that it was triggered by someone else's words, triggered from within. You recognize it immediately. Right on the spot, that anger gets transformed, undergoes metamorphosis.

Let us practice this awareness. The observer within us—the one who has the power to observe—all of us have an observer within, watching everything as a witness. We must cultivate that witness-consciousness.

Today, we are going to do this in our Seva Yoga as well. Speaking of Seva Yoga, a story comes to mind.

The war was over, and Lord Sri Ramachandra returned to Ayodhya. Rama's kingdom was running smoothly and happily. There was peace, supreme peace; everyone was happy, and the entire kingdom was prosperous with all abundance. At that time, everyone came and made a request to Lord Rama. They said:

“Give us some work to do. You are the king now. During the war with Ravana, we were able to offer our assistance and our services. But now we have no work. We feel restless; please give us some service.”

Then Lord Sri Ramachandra pondered over it, because the entire monkey army had performed some service or other. Starting from Sugriva, Hanumanji, Nila, and many other stalwarts—great warriors like Jambavan—all of them had rendered immense service.

So they requested Ramachandra Prabhu again, asking, Give us some work. Well, Rama called his minister and said:

“This request has come. What kinds of work are available? What departments are there in Seva Yoga?”

As a king ruling a kingdom, there are countless tasks, countless services to be rendered. So the minister wrote down a list of two to three hundred tasks and handed them out to everyone. Nila, your duty is to gently wave a fan for Lord Sri Ramachandra whenever there is no breeze. That is your duty, your service. This is Ram karyam. One by one, everyone took up their tasks. Two hundred tasks, three hundred tasks were assigned to everyone.

However, Hanumanji arrived a little late. But he came running anxiously, rushed up to Lord Ramachandra, and cried out:

“Oh my Lord, grant me too a share of service, the fortune of service!”

By then, however, the entire list was exhausted. All delegations and works had been assigned to everyone. Then Hanuman asked Rama:

“What about me? I love my Rama so much. I love you so much that I will do whatever work, whatever task you give me in your Ram karyam. It does not matter whether it is small or big; just give me any work. That is Ram karyam, and I will do it.”

Rama called the minister and asked if there was any other work left. They checked everything over again, and the minister finally said:

“There is only one single task. Normally, when we yawn, what do we do? We snap our fingers with the little finger while yawning. Your duty when Prabhu yawns is simply to snap your fingers—that is your job!”

Hanumanji did not think of it as small or big; he joined his hands in thanks, thinking:

“Oh, I have attained the fortune of rendering service! This is not big or small; I will gladly do this job of finger-snapping.”

Accepting it, he began to ponder. What if Prabhu gets yawns even while he is asleep? What should I do at that time? I cannot stay in his bedroom, in his resting place. Thinking that he must remain vigilant constantly, Hanumanji sat down under a tree and thought about it. An idea came to him: In that case, I will just keep snapping my fingers 24 hours a day, so that Prabhu never experiences any discomfort. This is my duty, my responsibility. This has been assigned as a great service task, and I will do it.

Having made this decision, he sat under that tree and kept snapping his fingers. He just kept snapping them. Meanwhile, Lord Ramachandra thought to himself: Hanumanji is my dearest disciple, my dearest devotee. The moment he snaps his little finger, I must yawn. He has decided that whenever he snaps his fingers there, I should yawn here.

So now, while he sat under the tree, Rama was holding court as king, performing his duties in the assembly hall. But Rama kept his mouth open! He kept yawning. Before he could close his mouth, a snap was made right there! Down in the assembly, the ministers and everyone else looked on and thought:

“Lord Ramachandra has contracted some illness. He keeps his mouth open!”

Two or three days passed, and he was not closing his mouth. They summoned all the physicians and held various consultations. Finally, they went and asked sage Vashistha. Since he was Rama's guru, the guru would know. They went and asked him.

The guru smiled and said:

“This is very simple. Did you give any duty to Anjaneya Swami? The duty of finger-snapping! That is the matter. Just go and tell Anjaneya Swami about this, and relieve him from it. Give him some other duty.”

Look at that! How much Hanumanji loved Ram karyam—the work of Rama.

That is why we do not need to ponder whether a task is small or large. It is all about the quality we bring to it—being completely immersed in it with awareness.
